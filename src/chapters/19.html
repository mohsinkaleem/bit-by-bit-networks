Rush hour in the dorm

Alice’s university room has Wi-Fi, and every morning it’s wonderful. Every evening, when two hundred students get back from lectures and start streaming, gaming and video calling their families, it’s hopeless. She rings Bob, who is feeling rather pleased with his new cable.

A: My Wi-Fi says five bars, and it’s slower than your old flashlight. B: Five bars is a strong signal. So it can’t be the radio? A: The signal isn’t the problem. The air is. There’s only so much of it, and everyone in this building is trying to talk into it at once. B: Like the shared wire in Chapter 8! Everyone on one cable, taking turns. A: Worse. On a cable you can at least hear a collision happening. In the air, you can’t.

Wi-Fi is Chapter 8’s shared cable, reborn as radio. Every device on a channel shares the same slice of the air, and only one can usefully talk at a time. It’s officially called IEEE 802.11, and “Wi-Fi” is the name its industry group chose in 1999, because it sounded friendlier. (It doesn’t stand for anything.) This chapter looks at how Wi-Fi takes turns, finds room in the air, and lets a laptop join a network securely.

Why the air is harder

On Chapter 8’s cable, a computer listened while it talked, and if the voltage looked wrong, it knew there’d been a collision and stopped at once. A radio can’t do that. Its own transmission, a few centimeters from its own antenna, is millions of times louder than anyone else’s, so while it’s talking it’s deaf to everything else. A Wi-Fi card can’t detect collisions. It has to avoid them, and when it can’t, find out afterwards.

The method is called CSMA/CA: carrier sense, multiple access, with collision avoidance. It works like a polite dinner party:

  • Listen first. If anyone is talking, wait.
  • Don’t all pounce at once. When the channel goes quiet, everyone who was waiting would otherwise start together, so each one first counts down a random number of 9-microsecond slots.
  • Every frame is acknowledged. The receiver sends a tiny ACK straight back. No ACK means something went wrong, probably a collision, so try again.
  • Back off harder each time. After a failure, pick the random number from a range twice as big: 0 to 15 slots, then 31, 63, and up to 1,023. This is Chapter 8’s exponential backoff, used before sending instead of after a crash.

It works, but it gets less efficient as more devices join in. The more of them there are, the more likely two pick the same random number:

Stations that can’t hear each other

Try the “can’t hear each other” box. Listening before talking only works if everyone can hear everyone, and in a house full of walls, they often can’t:

RTS and CTS cost a little time for every frame, so devices usually only bother for big frames, or when they notice lots of their frames going missing. There’s a clever detail in every Wi-Fi frame that makes this work: a duration field, saying how many microseconds the whole exchange will take, including the ACK. Anyone who hears a frame, even one that isn’t for them, stays quiet for that long. It’s called the network allocation vector, and it means a device can know the air is busy without actually hearing anyone.

The slow guest at the party

Now try the “far away” box. Device A is at the bottom of the garden, and its signal is so weak that it has to use the sparsest constellation (Chapter 17), so each of its frames takes eight times longer to send. CSMA/CA gives every device an equal chance to send a frame, not an equal share of time. So device A delivers about as many frames as the others, but hogs most of the air doing it, and everyone’s Wi-Fi slows to a crawl.

Bob’s mum watches videos on an old tablet at the bottom of the garden, where the Wi-Fi is weak. Every time she does, the voice chat on Bob’s laptop breaks up, even though her videos and his chat have nothing to do with each other. Why?

Because they share the same air. The tablet is far away, so it uses the slowest, sparsest constellation, and each of its frames takes many times longer to send. CSMA/CA gives it just as many turns as Bob’s laptop, so most of the airtime goes to the tablet, and the laptop spends its time waiting. Network problems are rarely where you first look, which is why Chapter 38 is all about being a detective.

Three addresses

An Ethernet frame has two addresses, “to” and “from”. A Wi-Fi frame usually has three, and here’s why:

The access point is a bridge (Chapter 18): Bob’s laptop is really sending to the router, but in the air, the frame has to be addressed to the access point, which is the only thing that will pick it up and pass it on. So a Wi-Fi frame carries the next hop in the air, the sender, and the final destination, plus two bits saying which way it’s going (“to the network” or “from the network”). The access point decrypts it, checks it, and sends it on as an ordinary Ethernet frame with just two addresses.

That’s why tcpdump on a Wi-Fi card shows ordinary Ethernet frames: the card does the same translation itself, before the tap ever sees them.

Finding room in the air

Wi-Fi’s 2.4 GHz band is divided into 13 channels (11 in the US), numbered every 5 MHz. But each channel is 20 MHz wide, so neighboring channels overlap a lot. Only channels 1, 6 and 11 are far enough apart to leave each other alone. Here’s what Grandpa Otto’s Pi could hear from Bob’s house one evening:

Nine networks in 2.4 GHz, from Bob’s house and the neighbors’. Everyone on channel 6 shares the air politely, using CSMA/CA, because they can decode each other’s frames and wait their turn. But the network on channel 3 is the worst possible neighbor: its signal overlaps channels 1 and 6, but can’t be decoded on either of them, so it just looks like noise to them. They can’t wait politely for it, and it can’t wait for them.

The 5 GHz and 6 GHz bands have far more room, so their channels can be joined together into much wider ones: 40, 80, 160 and even 320 MHz. Bob’s router uses the 80 MHz block from channel 149 to 161, which it shares with one neighbor. Wider channels carry more bits (Shannon, again), but they also give the neighbors more to collide with.

Those top speeds need every trick at once, and nobody gets them at home. But you can work out your own link’s speed exactly. Bob’s laptop reported 702 Mb/s on Wi-Fi 5 in Chapter 17. Here’s where that number comes from:

OFDM (“orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing”) splits one wide channel into hundreds of narrow subcarriers, each sending its own slow stream of QAM symbols. A slow symbol is much less bothered by echoes from walls, which arrive a fraction of a microsecond late and would otherwise smear one fast symbol into the next. Wi-Fi 6 goes one step further with OFDMA: the access point can give different groups of subcarriers to different devices in the same transmission, so ten phones each sending a tiny message don’t need ten separate turns. That’s exactly what a crowded dorm needs.

Joining a network

When Alice opens her laptop in the library, it finds the university’s network and connects in well under a second. Here’s what happened:

The key handshake is the clever part. The password never goes over the air, not even scrambled. Instead, both sides turn the password into a long key, mix it with two fresh random numbers and both devices’ addresses, and get a new key that belongs to this laptop, on this connection, alone. Then each proves to the other that it got the same result. That’s why, back in Chapter 16, a sniffer in monitor mode could hear everyone’s frames but read none of them: each laptop’s frames are locked with its own key.

There’s a catch, though. Anyone who records a handshake can take it home and try millions of guesses at the password, checking each one against the recording. A short password falls quickly. WPA3, from 2018, uses a different exchange (called SAE, or “dragonfly”) where a recording is useless, and every guess needs a new live conversation with the access point. We’ll see how clever exchanges like these work in Chapter 31.

Wi-Fi descends from ALOHAnet, the radio network from Hawaii in Chapter 8, and from a 1985 decision by the US government to let anyone use a few “junk” radio bands without a license. The 802.11 committee met for the first time in 1990, chaired by Vic Hayes, often called the father of Wi-Fi, and published the first standard in 1997, at 2 megabits per second.

Wi-Fi’s security has a bumpy history. The original scheme, WEP, was so badly designed that by 2001 its key could be recovered by listening for a few minutes. WPA replaced it as an emergency fix in 2003, and WPA2 arrived in 2004. In 2017, a researcher named Mathy Vanhoef found a way to trick devices into reusing keys during the four-way handshake (the “KRACK” attack), which was fixed with software updates, and helped push WPA3 along.

Listening to the air

To see the frames in this chapter for yourself (beacons, ACKs, RTS, the handshake), you need a card in monitor mode, which passes up every frame it hears, with a “radiotap” header of radio details in front: channel, signal strength and speed. On a Mac, hold the Option key, click the Wi-Fi icon in the menu bar, choose Open Wireless Diagnostics, then from its Window menu choose Sniffer. Pick a channel and press Start, and it saves a capture file in /var/tmp. (Your Wi-Fi is disconnected while it listens.) On Linux it depends on the card: many USB Wi-Fi adapters support monitor mode, but the Raspberry Pi’s built-in one doesn’t. The lab includes a sample capture, and a decoder for it.

Hidden stations waste more than half their attempts, until RTS/CTS makes their collisions short and cheap. And one slow device takes 78% of the air to deliver about a quarter of the frames. Wi-Fi 6’s OFDMA, and “airtime fairness” in better access points, exist to fix exactly this.

Experiments

  • If you have a Mac, capture a minute of your own channel with the Wireless Diagnostics sniffer, convert it with tcpdump -r in.pcap -w out.pcap if needed, and run python3 air.py out.pcap --networks. How many networks can you hear? How many retries do you see?
  • In wifi_sim.py, change CW_MIN from 15 to 3, then to 63. Which works better for 2 stations? For 50? Why can’t one number suit both?
  • Make Wi-Fi 6’s OFDMA idea into a new mode: instead of one device per turn, let up to four devices whose countdowns reach zero in the same slot share the turn. How much does it help the 50-station case?
  • With the Pi, run iw dev wlan0 station dump twice, a minute apart. How many frames did it fail to send (tx failed)? Do it again while something is streaming nearby.
