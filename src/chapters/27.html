Sent twice, lost once

Back in Chapter 16, the night Bob learned to use tcpdump, he also captured Grandpa Otto’s Raspberry Pi fetching https://example.org. He kept the file. Over the holidays, he and Alice finally sit down to read it, all 44 TCP segments of it.

B: Look at the sixth one. The Pi sends exactly the same 183 bytes it sent 65 milliseconds earlier. A: A retransmission. It thought the first one was lost. B: But it wasn’t! The server acknowledged it a moment later, and then it said it got them twice. And then the server did the same thing, and then the Pi did it again. A: And further down, something really was lost. Look at the gap in the numbers. B: So how does anyone know what’s lost and what’s just slow? A: That’s most of what TCP is for. We invented half of it in Chapter 13. Let’s see how the real thing does it.

In Chapter 13, Alice and Bob invented reliable delivery on top of an unreliable network: numbering, acknowledgements, a sliding window, timeouts, a handshake to start and a goodbye to finish. The real version is the Transmission Control Protocol, and it carries most of what you do online: web pages, email, file transfers, remote logins. This chapter takes it apart using Bob’s capture, one real segment at a time. The next chapter is about the other half of TCP: how fast to send.

Why random starting numbers, rather than starting at 0? Two reasons. A connection might be closed and reopened between the same two ports, and a delayed segment from the old one must not be mistaken for part of the new one. And an attacker who can guess the numbers can inject fake segments into someone else’s connection, which is exactly what a famous attack did in 1994, when starting numbers were predictable. So every modern operating system picks them with a secret-keyed hash that nobody outside can predict.

Counting bytes

TCP numbers bytes, not segments, exactly as Chapter 13 did. Once the handshake is done, the Pi’s first segment carries bytes 1 to 1388, its next bytes 1389 to 1571, and the server’s acknowledgement of 1572 means “I have everything before byte 1572; send 1572 next”. The data doesn’t have to be split the same way each time it’s sent, and the receiver doesn’t care how it was split: to the programs at each end, it’s one long stream of bytes. That’s why you can’t use TCP to send separate messages without marking where each one ends yourself (Chapter 5’s length prefixes):

$ python3 tcp_lab.py stream three sends of 4, 4 and 5 bytes; the other end's first recv() got: b'one two three'

Lost, or just slow?

Here’s the hard part. When a sender hears nothing back, it can’t tell whether its segment was lost, or the acknowledgement was, or both are just taking the scenic route. TCP has three ways to decide.

The oldest is the retransmission timeout, the one we invented in Chapter 13: if nothing is acknowledged in time, send it again. TCP measures every round trip (the timestamps option makes that easy: each segment carries the time it was sent, and the other side echoes it back), keeps a running average and a measure of how much it varies, and sets the timeout to the average plus four times the variation, the very formula from Chapter 13. It never goes below a minimum, 200 milliseconds on Linux and a whole second in the standard, and it doubles after every timeout, because if the network is in trouble, sending more makes it worse.

Waiting that long wastes time, so the second way is quicker. When a segment is lost but later ones arrive, the receiver acknowledges each of them with the same number, the first byte it’s missing. Three of these duplicate ACKs in a row are a strong hint, and the sender resends straight away, without waiting for the timer: that’s fast retransmit. The third way deals with the case the others can’t: when the lost segment is the last one, and nothing comes after it to trigger any duplicate ACKs. That’s what happened three times in this capture, and each time, nothing was actually lost:

Modern Linux goes further, with a method called RACK: rather than counting duplicate ACKs, it notices when a segment that was sent later has been acknowledged, and a segment sent earlier hasn’t, even after allowing for a little reordering. Then the earlier one is probably lost. It uses time, not counting, which works much better when several segments are lost at once.

Why were there so many unnecessary resends? Look at the timings: the server’s acknowledgements took 60 to 80 milliseconds to come back, on a connection whose round trip was 30. Something on the way was holding packets up, and that something turns out to be the subject of Chapter 28: when a link is busy, packets wait in a queue. Every one of those resends was TCP trying to be quick, and a network being slow.

If TCP can recover from anything, why did PSH appear on some segments? And what are URG and the urgent pointer for?

PSH (“push”) marks the end of a burst of data from the program, telling the receiver to hand it over now rather than wait for more. Most receivers hand data over straight away anyway, so it’s mostly a hint. URG was meant for “out-of-band” data, like pressing Ctrl-C in a remote login, that should jump the queue. Different systems implemented it in incompatible ways, and today it’s essentially never used: the urgent pointer in Bob’s SYN, like nearly every segment ever sent, is 0.

Goodbye

Closing a connection is a conversation of its own. Each direction is closed separately: a FIN means “I have nothing more to send”, but the side that sends it can keep receiving. (A program can do this on purpose, with shutdown(), to say “that’s my whole request” and then wait for the answer.) In Bob’s capture, the Pi sent its FIN first, at 382.9 ms, the server finished sending and sent its own at 459.9 ms, and each acknowledged the other’s.

The side that closed first waits in TIME_WAIT: 127.0.0.1.52667 127.0.0.1.7127 ⟦3:TIME_WAIT⟧ 127.0.0.1.7127 *.* LISTEN 1 | The client’s FIN has been acknowledged. It’s waiting for the server’s. 2 | The server has received the FIN, and is waiting for its program to close too. A server with thousands of connections in CLOSE_WAIT has a bug: it’s forgetting to close them. 3 | The connection is finished, but the client’s end hangs around, remembering it.

Why wait in TIME_WAIT? Because the very last ACK might be lost. If it is, the other side will send its FIN again, and someone has to be there to acknowledge it, rather than answering with an error. And because a stray, delayed segment from this connection could still be wandering the network, and must die of old age before the same pair of ports is used again. So the side that closed first waits for twice the longest time a segment could survive: a minute, on Linux.

There’s one more way for a connection to end: abruptly, with a reset. A segment with the RST flag means “I have no idea what you’re talking about: forget this connection”. It’s what a computer sends when a SYN arrives for a port nobody’s listening on, which is where “connection refused” comes from:

$ python3 tcp_lab.py refused ConnectionRefusedError(61, 'Connection refused') after 0.32 ms The other end's kernel answered our SYN with RST (“reset”): nobody is listening on that port.

It’s also what a program sends when it crashes with connections open, and, less innocently, what some firewalls forge to cut connections they don’t approve of.

A server has to remember every half-open connection (a SYN received, a SYN-ACK sent, no ACK yet) while it waits for the final ACK. What if someone sends it millions of SYNs, from fake addresses, and never answers?

That’s a SYN flood, one of the oldest attacks on the internet: the server’s memory fills up with half-open connections, and real visitors can’t get in. The clever defence is the SYN cookie: when it’s under attack, the server remembers nothing at all, and instead encodes everything it needs to know (a secret hash of the addresses, ports and time, plus the MSS) into its own starting sequence number. A real client’s ACK will acknowledge that number plus one, so the server can check it, and rebuild the connection from it. Fake addresses never send the ACK, and cost nothing.

TCP started as one protocol, described by Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn in a 1974 paper, that did everything: addresses, routing, reliability. In 1978 it was split in two, so that programs that didn’t want reliability (like voice) could use the routing on its own: that’s where IP, and later UDP, came from, and why people still say “TCP/IP”. The standard was published in 1981, and was finally rewritten, forty-one years and dozens of additions later, in 2022. SACK arrived in 1996, and tail loss probes and RACK in the 2010s, from Google, which studied why web pages loaded slowly and found that many of the delays were TCP waiting for timeouts.

What’s next?

We’ve seen how TCP makes sure everything arrives. But we haven’t seen how it decides how much to send at once. Send too little, and a fast connection sits idle; send too much, and the queues fill up, packets are lost, and everyone slows down, which may be exactly what Bob’s capture shows. Chapter 28 is about the two controls on TCP’s speed: flow control, which protects the receiver, and congestion control, which protects the network. And about why Bob’s games lag every evening.
