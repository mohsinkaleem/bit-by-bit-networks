A second way out

After the digger, Carol’s next project is the one she’s been asking for for years. The whole university reaches the internet through one connection, to the national research network. If that fails, twenty thousand students are cut off. So the university is buying a second connection, from a commercial internet provider called Northnet, and Alice gets to help.

C: The easy bit is the cable. The hard bit is telling the rest of the internet that it can reach us two ways. B: Can’t your routers just run OSPF with Northnet’s? Share the map? C: Would you give the neighbours a plan of your house, with where you keep the keys? Northnet has thousands of customers. It isn’t going to show us its network, and we don’t want every router in it knowing ours. A: And they don’t choose the shortest way, do they? They choose the cheapest. C: Exactly. So between networks, there’s a different protocol. Every network says which addresses it can reach, and which networks it would pass through to get there. And then each one decides for itself which offer to believe. B: Decides how? C: Mostly? By who’s paying.

This chapter is about the Border Gateway Protocol, BGP, which connects the internet’s separate networks to each other. Every other protocol in this book is about getting packets somewhere. BGP is about agreements: who will carry whose traffic, and who pays. It has almost no security, it’s driven by business deals nobody publishes, and it holds the whole internet together.

Networks of networks

The internet isn’t one network. It’s tens of thousands of them, each run by one organization: internet providers, universities, big companies, cloud providers, governments. Each one is called an autonomous system, or AS, because inside it, it can run things however it likes, with OSPF or anything else (Chapter 23). To the outside world, each one has a number. CERN’s is 513:

$ whois -h whois.cymru.com " -v 188.184.67.127" ⟦1:AS⟧ | IP | ⟦2:BGP Prefix⟧ | CC | Registry | Allocated | AS Name 513 | 188.184.67.127 | 188.184.0.0/16 | CH | ripencc | 2009-06-09 | CERN - CERN - European Organization for Nuclear Research, CH $ whois -h whois.cymru.com " -v 1.1.1.1" 13335 | 1.1.1.1 | 1.1.1.0/24 | AU | apnic | 2011-08-11 | CLOUDFLARENET - Cloudflare, Inc., US 1 | The autonomous system that announces this address to the rest of the internet. 2 | The route it announces: the whole of 188.184.0.0/16, which the info.cern.ch server from Chapter 16 is part of.

AS numbers used to be 16 bits, which allows 65,536 of them. Like IPv4 addresses, they began to run out, and since 2007 they’ve been 32 bits. And like addresses, some are set aside: 64512 to 65534 are for private use, and 64496 to 64511 are for examples in books, which is where most of this chapter’s come from. Here’s the size of the whole thing today, from this chapter’s lab, which asks RIPE’s route collectors (servers with BGP sessions to hundreds of networks around the world, which record everything they’re told):

Almost a million IPv4 routes, as Chapter 23 promised. Every router in the core of the internet has all of them.

Paths, not distances

BGP is a close relative of the distance-vector routing we invented in Chapter 11: each network tells its neighbours what it can reach, and they pass it on. But instead of a distance, each announcement carries the whole list of networks it has passed through:

The AS path does three jobs at once. It stops loops, since nobody accepts a route that already has their own number in it. It gives a rough idea of distance, since a shorter path usually means fewer networks in the way. And it tells every network exactly whose hands its traffic would pass through, which matters a great deal when those hands belong to competitors, or to a country you don’t trust.

Notice what BGP doesn’t say: anything about the inside of each network. An AS path of three numbers might cross three countries or three buildings. That’s on purpose. Each network hides its internal map, and only says which addresses it can reach, and through whom.

Follow the money

Which routes a network passes on, and to whom, is decided by business relationships, and there are two main kinds:

Peering happens in two ways: with a private cable between two networks, or at an internet exchange point, a building (often a big room full of switches) where hundreds of networks plug into one shared Ethernet, and set up BGP sessions with each other across it. The biggest, in Frankfurt, Amsterdam and London, carry more traffic than most countries’ entire internet connections.

These deals are private. Nobody publishes who pays whom, although it can often be guessed from the routes. Some networks do publish their policies, in public databases called Internet Routing Registries. Here’s a piece of CERN’s:

$ whois -h whois.ripe.net -r AS513 aut-num: AS513 as-name: CERN import: ⟦1:from AS174 accept ANY⟧ import: from AS293 accept ANY import: from AS559 accept ANY import: ⟦2:from AS1103 accept AS1103⟧ … export: ⟦3:to AS174 announce AS-CERNEXT⟧ … 1 | CERN accepts every route Cogent (AS174) offers: Cogent is one of its transit providers, and sells it a route to everywhere. 2 | From SURF, the Dutch research network, CERN only accepts SURF’s own addresses. That’s a peering arrangement. 3 | And to everyone, it announces only its own networks (a list called AS-CERNEXT). Its policy lists 48 neighbours.

Choosing a route

A network with several neighbours usually hears several routes to the same prefix, and picks exactly one, its best, which is the only one it uses and the only one it passes on. BGP routers compare routes in a fixed order, stopping at the first thing that’s different:

  1. Local preference: a number the network sets itself, by policy. Almost everyone uses it for money: routes from customers (who pay us) get the highest, then peers (free), then providers (who we pay).
  2. The shortest AS path.
  3. A few tie-breakers: where the route originally came from, a hint from the neighbour about which of its entrances to use (the “MED”), and routes from outside the network over ones from inside it.
  4. The closest exit: of two routes through different neighbours, the one whose border router is fewest internal hops away, using the OSPF costs from Chapter 23. Get the traffic out of our network as soon as possible, and let someone else carry it. It’s called hot-potato routing.
  5. If everything else is equal, the neighbour with the lowest router ID.

Notice the order. Money comes first, and path length only second. A network will happily send traffic the long way round, through a customer, rather than the short way through a provider it has to pay. Here are ten networks following these rules. Steering the traffic that comes in

Choosing a route only controls the traffic a network sends. What about the traffic coming in? Carol’s university wants its research traffic to arrive through the research network, which it pays a flat fee for, and only use Northnet, which charges by the gigabyte, as a backup. But it doesn’t get to choose: every other network picks its own best route.

It can nudge them, though. The simplest trick is AS path prepending: when announcing its addresses to Northnet, the university puts its own number in the path two or three times. Anyone comparing a path through Northnet with a path through the research network sees the Northnet one as longer, and, all else being equal, chooses the other. Try it in the widget above. (And notice that it doesn’t always work: prepending only matters when the local preferences are equal, and money comes first.)

There are more tricks. A network can announce more specific prefixes through the connection it prefers: longest-prefix match beats everything, so traffic for those addresses will always come that way. (That’s one reason the internet’s table keeps growing.) And it can attach communities to a route, little numbered tags like 174:21101, which ask the neighbour to do something with it: “don’t pass this on in Asia”, “prepend three times when you tell your peers”, or, in an emergency, “throw away all traffic for this address”, which is how networks stop a flood of attack traffic from reaching a victim (at the cost of cutting the victim off completely).

What BGP says on the wire

Compared with OSPF, the protocol itself is refreshingly simple. Two BGP routers open an ordinary TCP connection to port 179 (Chapter 13 does all the hard work of reliability for it), and exchange just four kinds of message. This chapter’s lab has a tiny BGP speaker. It plays both the university and Northnet, on your own computer, and shows the conversation from the university’s side:

A provider can instead send its customer the full table of a million routes. 5 | An empty update says “that’s everything, for now”. After this, only changes are sent. 6 | “Send traffic for these addresses to 10.0.1.1”: the university’s end of the cable between them. 7 | A withdrawal: “I can’t reach this any more”. Every router that chose this route has to pick another one, and tell its neighbours.

BGP doesn’t send its routes over and over like RIP, or refresh them every half hour like OSPF. It sends everything once, when the session starts, and after that only the changes. That’s efficient, but it means that when a session breaks and comes back, a million routes have to be sent again, and every router that heard them has to think about all of them. The famous “BGP storms” are exactly that.

Inside a big network, there are usually several border routers, each with its own BGP sessions to the outside, and they need to tell each other what they’ve heard. They do it with BGP too, over sessions inside the network, called internal BGP (iBGP). OSPF carries the network’s own addresses, and iBGP carries the outside world’s routes on top. That’s how a router in the middle of Carol’s campus knows that traffic for CERN should leave through the research network’s border router, and OSPF tells it how to get to that router.

If announcing a more specific prefix always wins, why doesn’t every network announce a separate /32 for every one of its servers? Then nobody could ever out-announce them.

Because nobody would listen. A million routes are already a strain on routers’ memory, and a /32 for every server would be billions. So by long-standing agreement, almost every network ignores IPv4 announcements more specific than a /24 (and IPv6 ones more specific than a /48). A /24, 256 addresses, is the smallest piece of the internet you can route on its own. It also means that anyone who announces your /24 can only be beaten by… announcing it too, and hoping. Or by the protection in the next section.

When the treaty breaks

Here is BGP’s great weakness. When a network announces a prefix, its neighbours have no way to check that it’s allowed to. The protocol just believes it. Mistakes and lies spread across the internet in seconds:

That’s a hijack: announcing addresses that belong to somebody else. Most hijacks are mistakes, like a typo in a router’s configuration, but some are deliberate, to steal traffic, send spam from someone else’s addresses, or intercept cryptocurrency. Try it in the widget: choose “Eve also announces 10.1.1.0/24”, and watch almost every arrow turn red. Even the university itself sends traffic for its own library server to Eve, unless it has thought to filter out announcements of its own addresses.

The other common failure is a route leak, where a network breaks the export rules by accident. In June 2019, a small internet provider in Pennsylvania ran software that generated more specific routes for traffic engineering inside its own network. A configuration mistake sent them to one of its providers, the network of a metals company, which passed them to a much bigger one, which believed them, because they came from a customer and were more specific. For hours, a large share of traffic to Cloudflare, Amazon and others was squeezed through a small company’s connections, and most of it was lost. Choose the leak in the widget to see the same thing in miniature.

Signing the map: RPKI

The fix that’s finally spreading is the Resource Public Key Infrastructure, or RPKI. The organizations that hand out addresses (the regional registries, like RIPE NCC in Europe) give each address holder a cryptographic certificate saying which addresses are theirs. The holder uses it to sign a route origin authorization, or ROA: “only AS 513 may announce 188.184.0.0/16, and nothing longer than /16”. Routers download all the ROAs in the world, and check each announcement’s origin, the last number in its AS path, against them. The lab showed CERN’s: valid. Here are two checks of Cloudflare’s 1.1.1.0/24, one with its real AS, and one pretending to be a private AS that has no right to it:

$ curl -s "https://stat.ripe.net/data/rpki-validation/data.json?resource=13335&prefix=1.1.1.0/24" | python3 -c 'import json,sys; print(json.load(sys.stdin)["data"]["status"])' ⟦1:valid⟧ $ curl -s "https://stat.ripe.net/data/rpki-validation/data.json?resource=64512&prefix=1.1.1.0/24" | python3 -c 'import json,sys; print(json.load(sys.stdin)["data"]["status"])' ⟦2:invalid_asn⟧ 1 | Cloudflare’s own AS, 13335, is allowed to announce it. 2 | Any other origin is invalid. A network doing “route origin validation” throws such a route away. Eve’s /24 in the widget is invalid for a different reason: the university’s ROA says nothing longer than /16.

Tick the RPKI box in the widget. The tier-1 networks and Northnet now drop Eve’s route, which protects them, and everyone who reaches the university through them. But Southnet doesn’t check, so its customers are still fooled. RPKI only protects the networks that use it, and the ones behind them. Most of the world’s biggest networks now do, and more than half of all routes have ROAs.

RPKI only checks the origin, though. Eve could still announce a path that ends in the university’s number, “65066 65001”, and pass the check, since it can’t tell a real path from a made-up one. Newer additions deal with that: one lets networks sign each hop of the path (BGPsec, which is slow and rarely used), and another, called ASPA, lets each network publish a list of its providers, so that impossible paths can be spotted.

BGP was designed in January 1989, by Kirk Lougheed of Cisco and Yakov Rekhter of IBM, sketched out over lunch at an engineering meeting in Austin, Texas, on the back of a few napkins. It’s still sometimes called “the napkin protocol”. The version used today, BGP-4, arrived in 1994 with CIDR (Chapter 23), and the text that defines it was last revised in 2006.

On 4 October 2021, Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp disappeared from the internet for about six hours. During routine maintenance, a command accidentally cut the links between Facebook’s data centres. Facebook’s name servers are designed to stop announcing their own addresses by BGP when they can’t reach a data centre, as a safety measure, so they all did, at once. With no routes to Facebook’s name servers, nobody could look up facebook.com, including Facebook’s own engineers, whose tools and door badges depended on the same systems.

What’s next?

Both chapters about routing have been full of the same problem, seen from different sides: there aren’t enough IPv4 addresses. Networks announce tiny pieces of them, trade them, share them through NAT (Chapter 22), and the table keeps growing. The regional registries handed out their last big blocks between 2011 and 2019. The real fix was designed in the 1990s, and it’s been slowly taking over ever since: a new version of IP, with addresses four times as long. Chapter 25 is about IPv6.

Four programs. bgp_sim.py is the ten networks from the widget. asinfo.py asks RIPE’s route collectors about any address. bgp_speaker.py speaks real BGP. And bgp_box.sh (Linux, with sudo) runs five real BGP routers on one computer.
