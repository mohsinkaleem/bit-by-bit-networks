A few years later

Alice is away at university now, studying computer science. Bob still lives at home, and has somehow become the family’s IT department. He set up the home Wi-Fi, he fixes the printer, and last month he set up a little Raspberry Pi computer for Grandpa Otto, who wanted to learn to program. One evening, Alice’s phone rings.

B: The internet’s broken again. My game lags every single evening, and Mum says it’s my fault for downloading things. A: Is it the Wi-Fi? The router? Your internet provider? The game’s servers? B: That’s what I’m asking you! A: Then let’s stop guessing. Everything your computers say and hear goes through their network cards, one packet at a time. We can watch them. B: Watch them? Like… spying? A: Only on yourself. It’s your network, and your packets.

In Part I, we invented a network. In Part II, we investigate a real one: yours. Every chapter will take one piece of the real internet apart, and the first thing we need is a way to see it. That’s what this chapter is about: the tools that let you watch every packet go by, and a little language for picking out the ones you care about.

We met tcpdump briefly in Chapter 15. Now we’ll learn to use it properly, see how it works inside, and meet its big sibling, Wireshark.

Where the sniffer sits

A program like tcpdump is called a packet sniffer. It doesn’t sit on the wire, or in the air. It sits inside your own computer, at a spot called the tap, just above the network card’s driver:

Every packet that comes in or goes out passes the tap, and the tap hands a copy of each one to a tiny filter program. If the packet matches, the copy is put in a buffer for tcpdump to read. The original carries on as if nothing happened. So a sniffer can’t block, change or slow down your traffic. It only watches.

Watching needs administrator rights, because the tap sees everything: every program’s packets, including other users’ on the same computer. That’s why the commands in this chapter start with sudo. (On Linux, you can instead add yourself to the wireshark group, which lets Wireshark’s capture helper, dumpcap, capture without sudo. The Wireshark installer for macOS offers something similar.)

Two small things make this work. -n means “don’t turn addresses into names”. Without it, tcpdump would look up the name of every address it prints, which makes more packets, which appear in the capture. And not port 22 is a filter. Bob is logged in to the Pi over SSH, which uses port 22, so every line tcpdump prints travels to Bob’s screen as an SSH packet, which tcpdump would print, which would travel to Bob’s screen… The filter breaks the loop.

Each line is one packet, and they all follow the same pattern:

Look at the options on the two SYN packets. The Pi says mss 1460: “send me pieces of up to 1,460 bytes”. The answer from CERN says mss 1452. Remember the path MTU from Chapter 10’s lab, which was 1,492 bytes instead of 1,500 on this network. Can you connect the two numbers?

1,492 − 20 bytes of IP header − 20 bytes of TCP header = 1,452. Something on the way, almost certainly the home router, noticed that bigger pieces won’t fit through the internet connection, and quietly rewrote the number as the SYN went past. That’s called MSS clamping, and we’ll meet it again in Chapter 22.

Finding the needle

A busy computer sends and receives thousands of packets a second, so the real skill is in filtering. The expression after the options, like not port 22, is a capture filter, written in a small language of its own. Here are the words you’ll use most:

FilterKeeps packets…
host 192.168.1.1to or from that address
src host 192.168.1.4, dst host …from it, or to it
net 192.168.1.0/24to or from anything on that network (Chapter 20 explains the /24)
port 53, tcp port 80, udp dst port 123to or from that port
arp, icmp, tcp, udp, ip6of that protocol
ether host 2c:cf:67:5e:10:c2, broadcastto or from that card, or sent to everyone
greater 1000, less 100bigger or smaller than that
tcp[13] & 2 != 0whose TCP header has byte 13, bit 2 set: the SYN flag. Any byte of any header can be tested like this.
not, and, or, ( … )combine the others

Two things catch everyone out. First, the shell treats brackets and & specially, so put the whole filter in quotes: 'tcp[13] & 2 != 0'. Second, and and or have the same priority, and are read left to right. So udp or icmp and host 1.1.1.1 means (udp or icmp) and host 1.1.1.1, which is probably not what you meant. When in doubt, add brackets.

How the filter runs

The widget shows something odd: every filter turns into a short list of instructions like ldh [12] and jeq #0x800. That’s because the filter doesn’t run inside tcpdump. It runs inside the operating system’s kernel, at the tap, on every single packet, and it has to be very fast and completely safe. A buggy filter must never crash the computer.

So tcpdump compiles your filter into a program for a tiny, simple imaginary computer, the Berkeley Packet Filter, or BPF. It has just two registers (A and X), it can only read the packet, and it can only jump forwards, so every program is guaranteed to finish. The kernel checks the program once, then runs it on every packet. You can ask tcpdump to show you the program with -d. Here’s one, running on the Pi’s “GET /” packet:

Notice how much of the filter’s work is just finding things. Offset 12 is always the frame’s type field (Chapter 8), and offset 23 is always the IP header’s protocol field, but the TCP header can start at different places, so the program has to work it out from the IP header’s length. Every decoding tool does the same job, in the same way: read a field, then use it to decide how to read the next one.

tcpdump was written in 1988 by Van Jacobson, Craig Leres and Steven McCanne at the Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory. (Jacobson again: he also fixed TCP’s congestion collapse, in Chapter 13, and wrote traceroute.) The packet filter came a few years later, in a 1992 paper by McCanne and Jacobson. It was so much faster than the filters before it that it was adopted almost everywhere, and the capture part of tcpdump was split out into a library, libpcap, which nearly every capture tool still uses today.

In 1998 Gerald Combs started writing a graphical packet analyzer called Ethereal, renamed Wireshark in 2006. It can decode thousands of protocols. And BPF itself grew up: Linux turned it into eBPF, a general-purpose, safe way to run small programs inside the kernel, which now powers firewalls, load balancers and monitoring tools. That’s in Chapter 39.

Save it for later

Instead of printing packets, tcpdump can save them to a file with -w, to be read back, filtered and taken apart as often as you like with -r. Reading a file doesn’t need sudo:

$ sudo tcpdump -i wlan0 -w home.pcap not port 22 tcpdump: listening on wlan0, link-type EN10MB (Ethernet), snapshot length 262144 bytes ^C18 packets captured $ tcpdump -r home.pcap -n icmp reading from file home.pcap, link-type EN10MB (Ethernet), snapshot length 262144 23:04:18.843672 IP 192.168.1.4 > 1.1.1.1: ICMP echo request, id 15080, seq 1, length 64 23:04:18.857326 IP 1.1.1.1 > 192.168.1.4: ICMP echo reply, id 15080, seq 1, length 64 23:04:19.845376 IP 192.168.1.4 > 1.1.1.1: ICMP echo request, id 15080, seq 2, length 64 23:04:19.862256 IP 1.1.1.1 > 192.168.1.4: ICMP echo reply, id 15080, seq 2, length 64

This is the .pcap format we built by hand in Chapter 15. A few more options are worth knowing:

  • -s 128 keeps only the first 128 bytes of each packet. That’s enough for all the headers, and makes long captures much smaller. (It’s also more polite: you keep the envelopes, not the letters.)
  • -C 100 -W 10 writes a ring buffer: files of 100 megabytes, keeping only the newest 10. Leave it running for a week, and when the problem happens, the evidence is in the last file.
  • -U writes each packet as soon as it arrives, instead of in big batches. You’ll need it to pipe packets into another program, as in the lab.
  • -e prints each frame’s MAC addresses, and -X prints the bytes of each packet in hex.

Wireshark’s own format, pcapng (“next generation”), can also store comments, several interfaces, and which program sent each packet. Both are read by every tool in this chapter.

What can you see from here?

A sniffer only sees the packets that reach its own network card, and that depends on where the card is:

On an old hub, or a shared cable (Chapter 8), every frame reaches every card. Network cards normally ignore frames that aren’t addressed to them, but tcpdump switches on promiscuous mode, which tells the card to pass everything up. On a switch (Chapter 9), frames only go where they need to, so Bob sees his own traffic plus broadcasts like “who has?”, and nothing else. That’s why network engineers set up mirror ports on switches, which get a copy of everything, or put a network tap on a cable.

Wi-Fi is a shared medium again: every card within range hears every frame. But a card normally only passes up frames for itself, rewritten to look like Ethernet frames (that’s why tcpdump said EN10MB (Ethernet) on the Pi’s Wi-Fi). A card in monitor mode hears everything in the air, as it really is, but each laptop’s frames are encrypted with its own key. We’ll listen in properly in Chapter 19.

Bob’s little brother is streaming a film on the TV, which is plugged into the router by a cable. Bob runs tcpdump on his laptop, which is on Wi-Fi. What will he see of the film?

Nothing at all. The router has a switch inside, and the film’s packets go only to the TV’s port. Bob will only see broadcasts and multicasts, like “who has?” messages and devices announcing themselves. To see the TV’s traffic, he’d have to capture on the router itself, or on a mirror port.

A word of warning, which matters more than anything else in this chapter: only capture on networks you own, or have permission to watch. Capturing other people’s traffic without permission is illegal in most countries, even if you can’t read it. Luckily, your own computer is interesting enough for a lifetime.

Experiments

  • Real tcpdump understands port 80 or 443: a bare number after or means “the same kind of thing as before”. minidump.py says '443' isn't an IPv4 address. Teach the parser to remember the last keyword.
  • python3 minidump.py -d "ip and tcp dst port 80" makes 13 instructions, but tcpdump -d makes 11. Which instructions are wasted? Real tcpdump has an optimizer that removes tests whose answers are already known. Can you write a simple one?
  • Which packets have the “don’t chop me up” flag off? Try 'ip[6] & 0x40 = 0', and work out why those are the ones.
  • Capture your own traffic to a file with sudo tcpdump -i en0 -w mine.pcap while you browse for a minute. How many packets? Now filter for 'tcp[tcpflags] & tcp-syn != 0 and tcp[tcpflags] & tcp-ack = 0'. Each one is a new connection. How many did one web page open?
  • On Linux, try capturing with -i any and piping into minidump.py. It refuses. Look up “Linux cooked capture” to find out why, and what the frames look like instead.
