Grandpa goes viral

A local news programme did a piece on Grandpa Otto’s beans. At the end, the presenter read out the address of his website. Within a minute, the Pi’s little fan was screaming; within five, nobody could load anything.

B: Fifty thousand people want to see the beans, and there’s one Raspberry Pi. It can’t even accept the connections fast enough. A: Then don’t make them all come to the Pi. Most of them want exactly the same photos. Put copies closer to them. B: Copies where? We don’t have computers all over the country. A: Other people do. And while we’re at it: put something in front of the Pi that decides who gets through. Last night half of those “visitors” were Eve’s script, asking for the same page ten times a second. E: I was testing it. You’re welcome.

So far, most of Part II has pictured two computers talking to each other, with routers in between that just pass packets along. Real traffic hardly ever works like that. Between Bob and almost any website sit machines that do things with his requests: answer them on the website’s behalf, spread them across many servers, cache them, inspect them, or refuse them. This chapter meets the middlemen.

Proxies

A proxy takes a request, and makes it again, somewhere else, on your behalf. The same program can sit in two very different places:

A forward proxy works for a group of users: a school or a company sends all its web traffic through one, to cache popular pages, keep logs, or block sites. The browser is told about it (in its settings, or by a small configuration file), and sends its requests there instead of to the sites. For plain http://, the request line then carries the whole URL, and the proxy fetches it. For https://, the browser can’t let the proxy read anything, so it asks it to CONNECT: open a TCP connection to a host and port, and then pass bytes blindly in both directions. This chapter’s lab is a forward proxy that says what it can see:

$ python3 proxy.py --block example.com forward proxy on 127.0.0.1:7140. Try: curl -x http://127.0.0.1:7140 https://example.org/ 22:16:58 GET http://info.cern.ch/ → HTTP/1.1 200 OK, 878 bytes. ⟦1:I can read all of it (user-agent: curl/8.7.1)⟧ 22:16:58 CONNECT example.org:443 a tunnel: 585 bytes up, 5,003 down, 0.1 s. ⟦2:First bytes up were 160301…: a TLS hello.⟧ The rest is sealed. 22:16:58 CONNECT example.com:443 → ⟦3:403: blocked⟧ 1 | Plain HTTP: the proxy sees the whole request and response, and could change any of it. 2 | HTTPS: just a name, a port, and how many bytes went each way. The first bytes are a ClientHello (Chapter 32), and after that, sealed records. 3 | It can still refuse to connect, by name. That’s how most school and office filters work.

(Some companies go further, and install their own root certificate on their employees’ laptops. Then their proxy can pretend to be any website, decrypt the traffic, inspect it, and encrypt it again: Eve’s third trick from Chapter 32, done openly by the owner of the computer.)

A reverse proxy works for a website. Everyone on the internet connects to it, and it passes each request to one of the servers behind it. That lets the site add servers, take them out for repairs, and hide how many there are, without anyone noticing. When its main job is spreading the work, it’s called a load balancer.

Spreading the load

The lab’s balancer.py runs three copies of Grandpa’s site on one computer (bean-2 is slow, as if it were an old Pi) and a load balancer in front of them. It sends 30 requests, six at a time, like a browser, and tries three ways of choosing a server:

$ python3 balancer.py demo three servers: bean-1 and bean-3 answer in 10 ms, bean-2 takes 200 ms round-robin: 30 requests, 6 at a time bean-1: 10 bean-2: 10 bean-3: 10 ⟦1:average 85 ms⟧, slowest 221 ms least-conn: 30 requests, 6 at a time bean-1: 14 bean-2: 2 bean-3: 14 ⟦2:average 28 ms⟧, slowest 209 ms hash: 30 requests, 6 at a time bean-1: 0 bean-2: 12 bean-3: 18 average 92 ms, slowest 212 ms ⟦3:alice always went to: ['bean-2']⟧ bob always went to: ['bean-3'] carol always went to: ['bean-3'] 1 | Round robin takes turns: fair, simple, and blind. The slow server gets a third of the work anyway. 2 | Least connections sends each request to whichever server is busiest the least. The slow one stays busy longer, so it’s picked less: three times faster on average. 3 | Hashing the visitor’s address (Chapter 31’s fingerprints again) sends each visitor to the same server every time, which matters when a server remembers something about them. With only five visitors it’s lumpy: nobody happened to land on bean-1.

So when a website shows you a 502 or 504, it usually isn’t the website speaking: it’s a middleman, apologizing for it.

Closer to everyone: content delivery networks

A load balancer spreads the work across servers in one place. But Grandpa’s viewers are all over the country, and a round trip to the Pi costs each of them the same distance, every time. A content delivery network, or CDN, keeps copies of a site’s files in hundreds of places around the world, each a big reverse proxy with a cache that follows exactly Chapter 30’s rules. Here’s a real one, answering for python.org:

Two things make it work. The site hands over its DNS, so that its name leads to the CDN, and it hands over its TLS certificate (or lets the CDN get one), so that the CDN can end the encrypted connection and read the requests. That’s a lot of trust: Cloudflare, Fastly, Akamai and a few others can read a large share of the world’s web traffic, in exactly the way Eve couldn’t in Chapter 32. The rest of this chapter explains how they reach everyone quickly.

One address, many places

In Chapter 29, the resolver started every lookup at the root servers, whose thirteen addresses have barely changed in thirty years. But there aren’t thirteen machines: there are nearly two thousand. This chapter’s anycast.py asks each root server a special question, hostname.bind in DNS’s rarely-used CHAOS class, which makes it say which machine answered:

The names are how the operators know which copy answered. (Copies in cities near the Pi are blanked out as XXX.)

Every copy of a root server uses the same address. How? Remember BGP from Chapter 24: an autonomous system announces “I can reach this block of addresses”, and every network picks its favourite route. Nothing stops several places from announcing the same block. Each network then simply picks the route it likes best, usually the nearest:

That’s anycast, and it’s why 1.1.1.1, a single address, answers from about ten milliseconds away in hundreds of cities, and why the AS paths to it are so short: it’s announced right next to you. CDNs use it too, for the addresses their DNS hands out. It works best for short, stateless exchanges like DNS questions, because if the routes change halfway through a long TCP connection, the next packet may arrive at a different site, which has never heard of it.

Walls with doors: firewalls

Eve’s script was the other half of Grandpa’s problem. A firewall decides which packets may pass, by rules: from where, to where, which protocol and port. The simplest kind looks at each packet on its own. That turns out to be surprisingly hard to get right, because replies to your own requests come from outside too, and a rule that lets them in must guess what a reply looks like. The lab runs the same twelve packets through two firewalls:

Blocking these breaks traceroute, and worse (Chapter 38). 3 | The firewall with memory forgets a UDP “conversation” after 30 seconds of silence.

Every home router has a stateful firewall like this, and its NAT (Chapter 22) behaves like one anyway: a packet from outside that doesn’t match a mapping has nowhere to go. That’s why a laptop at home is quietly protected from Eve’s SSH attempts, and why Chapter 22 needed STUN tricks to let two computers behind NATs talk.

Surviving the flood

Firewalls block packets that are obviously unwanted. But Eve’s script sent perfectly ordinary requests, just far too many of them. That’s a denial-of-service attack, and when it comes from thousands of computers at once (usually hacked ones: routers, cameras, anything with a weak password) it’s a distributed one, a DDoS. No single firewall can stop a flood bigger than its own internet connection. What works is the rest of this chapter, combined: anycast spreads the flood over hundreds of sites, each big enough to absorb its share; the CDN answers from cache, so floods of the same page never reach the origin; and the reverse proxies limit how often each address may ask, answering 429 Too Many Requests to anyone who asks too often.

Grandpa’s site now lives behind a CDN. Fifty thousand people saw the beans, and the Pi served about forty requests.

Chapter 32 said HTTPS is encrypted from the browser to the server, and nobody in between can read it. But a CDN reads every request, and caches the answers. How can both be true?

Because TLS protects a connection to whoever holds the certificate’s private key, and the website chose to give that to the CDN. As far as TLS is concerned, the CDN’s edge is the website. It then makes a second connection, usually also encrypted, to the real server. That’s a big reason the web is more centralized than it looks: a handful of companies terminate TLS for a large part of it. The encryption between Bob and the site is real; it’s just that “the site” includes its CDN.

In the 1990s, a link from a popular page could bring down almost any website, a “flash crowd” (later nicknamed the Slashdot effect, after a news site whose links did it daily). Tom Leighton, an MIT mathematician, and his student Danny Lewin founded Akamai in 1998 to fix it, with clever algorithms for spreading copies of websites over thousands of servers. Lewin was killed on the first plane hijacked on 11 September 2001.

Packet-filtering firewalls appeared in the late 1980s. Stateful inspection arrived in 1994, with a product called FireWall-1. And in October 2016, a botnet called Mirai, made of hundreds of thousands of hacked security cameras and home routers, flooded the DNS provider Dyn, and made Twitter, Netflix, GitHub and many others unreachable for hours across much of North America and Europe: the websites were fine; nobody could look up their addresses.

What’s next?

Every middleman in this chapter sits on the path between Bob and a website. But what if Bob wants the path itself to be different? Grandpa wants to reach his Pi’s camera from Alice’s house, and it has a private address behind his router’s NAT. Bob’s school wants him to reach its internal network from home. The answer is to wrap packets inside other packets, and send them through tunnels. Chapter 35 builds a VPN.

Experiments

  • Make the hash strategy in balancer.py survive a server failing without moving everyone: when a server leaves, only its own visitors should move. (Look up consistent hashing, or “rendezvous hashing”, which is just a few lines.)
  • Add a rate limit to balancer.py: at most 5 requests a second from each address, and 429 Too Many Requests for the rest. How would you tell the client when to try again? (There’s a header for it.)
  • Give proxy.py a cache for plain HTTP, using Chapter 30’s cacheproxy.py. Why can’t it cache anything that goes through CONNECT?
  • In fw_box.sh, delete the ct state established,related rule. The Pi can no longer fetch anything from outside, but its web server still works. Why the difference? (Which port do the packets of each connection arrive on?)
