A letter to Mars

It’s late at Uncle Ray’s farm. The satellites of the last chapter are sliding silently overhead, and Grandpa Otto has found a news story on his phone: a rover on Mars has sent back a picture of a crater rim, taken that morning.

G: How do they talk to it? When I was a signalman, if the other ship didn’t answer, you flashed the message again. And again, until it did. B: By radio. Mars is fourteen minutes away today, at the speed of light. G: Fourteen minutes! You could boil an egg while you wait for “hello”. A: And that’s the easy part. The rover can hardly ever talk to Earth directly. An orbiter flies over it a couple of times a day, collects what it has to say, and hands it on when a big dish on Earth is pointed its way. G: So it’s like the post. You hand your letter to someone who carries it part of the way, and they hand it to someone else. B: Nothing like the internet, then. The internet needs the whole path to be there at once. A: Exactly. So for space, people invented a network that doesn’t.

Everything in this book so far has assumed two things quietly: that a round trip takes at most a second or so, and that there’s a path all the way from sender to receiver right now. In space, neither is true. This chapter is about networks that work anyway: delay-tolerant networking. And, it turns out, they’re useful on Earth too.

Too far for a conversation

How far is Mars? It depends on the day. Earth and Mars both go round the Sun, Earth faster, so the distance between them swings from about 55 to 400 million kilometres. This chapter’s mars.py works it out from the same kind of numbers as Chapter 40’s satellites, published by NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory for each planet. (Checked against JPL’s own precise calculations, it’s within 0.02%.)

What does a delay like that do to the protocols we know? Start with the very first step of any TCP connection (Chapter 27): the handshake. The lab’s mars_link.sh builds a “Mars” on Grandpa’s Pi: a namespace (Chapter 36) at the end of a virtual cable, with Linux’s network emulator, netem, holding every packet for 5 minutes and 38 seconds in each direction. That’s Mars at its very nearest.

Suppose TCP were patient enough. It would still be hopeless. Slow start (Chapter 28) doubles its window once per round trip, so reaching a useful speed would take hours. Every lost packet would cost at least eleven minutes to notice. A TLS handshake (Chapter 32) adds another round trip, and a web page (Chapter 37) half a dozen more: an hour to load a page, if nothing went wrong. The internet’s protocols are built on conversation, and at these distances there’s no such thing.

Connected, some of the time

Distance isn’t even the biggest problem. A rover’s radio is small and it has little power to spare. It can talk to Earth directly, slowly, but most of what it sends goes by way of the spacecraft orbiting Mars, which fly over it for ten minutes or so, a couple of times a Martian day. The orbiters have big antennas and plenty of power, and they talk to Earth through NASA’s Deep Space Network: three sites with dishes up to 70 metres across, in California, Spain and Australia, a third of the way round the world from each other, so one of them can always see Mars. But the dishes serve dozens of missions, so each gets its own booked hours.

So the path from Earth to a rover is a chain of links that come and go on a timetable. There is no moment when the whole path exists at once. That isn’t a fault to be fixed: it’s how the network is.

Store, carry and forward

The answer is to stop having conversations and start sending letters. In delay-tolerant networking (DTN), an application hands the network a complete message, a bundle, with everything needed to deliver it: where it’s going, who sent it, when it was made, and how long it stays useful. Each node along the way takes the bundle, stores it, on disk if need be, for minutes or days, and forwards it when a link to the next node opens. Some nodes carry it, physically: an orbiter holding a rover’s photos until it’s in sight of Earth.

Three ideas make it work:

  • Hop by hop, not end to end. Each link only has to work for its own short hop, while it’s up. No handshake ever crosses the whole path.
  • Routing by timetable. Spacecraft move predictably (Chapter 40), so the times when links will be up, the contact plan, are known days ahead. Routes are worked out through time as well as space: “give it to the orbiter now; it will meet the rover at 09:20.”
  • Whatever link works. Bundles can travel over TCP or UDP where there’s an ordinary network, and over a protocol called LTP across deep space. These are “convergence layers”: the bundle layer sits on top of whichever one each hop uses, just as IP sits on top of Ethernet or Wi-Fi (Chapter 15).

The Bundle Protocol, version 7, was published as RFC 9171 in 2022. Bundles are written in CBOR (Concise Binary Object Representation): think of JSON (Chapter 30), but in compact binary. Why not just fix TCP: give it longer timers, and bigger windows?

Some of that was tried, and helps for the Moon (a second and a quarter each way). But TCP’s real assumption is deeper: that sender and receiver can talk while the data moves, so the receiver’s acknowledgements can steer the sender (Chapters 13 and 28). With no path end to end, there’s no one to steer. So DTN moves the responsibility to each hop: a node that has taken a bundle keeps it until the next node has it safely. Reliability becomes a relay race instead of a phone call. The same idea is in email: mail servers pass messages along, and keep them until the next one can take them.

Vint Cerf, one of TCP/IP’s inventors (Chapter 27), began working on an “interplanetary internet” with NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory in 1998. In 2003, Kevin Fall generalised the idea into delay-tolerant networking for any “challenged” network. In 2008 NASA tested it on a real spacecraft, the Deep Impact comet probe, about 24 million kilometres from Earth, and DTN has been used on the International Space Station since 2016. Plans for networks around the Moon, like NASA’s LunaNet, are built on the Bundle Protocol.

On Earth, DTN helps wherever connections come and go. In the early 2000s, DakNet put Wi-Fi boxes on rural buses in India, which collected villages’ email as they passed and delivered it when they reached a town. Researchers built DTN for Sámi reindeer herders in the far north of Sweden, who spend months beyond any network. And the biggest data transfers of all are still delay-tolerant: cloud companies ship disks by lorry, because, as the computer scientist Andrew Tanenbaum put it, you should never underestimate the bandwidth of a station wagon full of tapes hurtling down the highway.

What’s next?

The rover’s messages are protected, of course: nobody wants a stranger commanding a car on Mars. But Eve has been reading about something that worries everyone who keeps secrets for a long time. She doesn’t need to break today’s encryption today. She just needs to keep a copy, and wait for a computer that can.

Three programs. mars.py and dtn.py run on macOS or Linux. mars_link.sh is for Linux, needs sudo, and takes about twelve minutes: time for a cup of tea.

Experiments

  • With mars_link.sh 60, a round trip takes two minutes, just under TCP’s patience. Does curl manage to connect? Does it get the page? What about 70 seconds?
  • Give each bundle a lifetime of 12 hours instead of a week (lifetime_hours), and make nodes throw away bundles that have expired. What happens to the rover’s answer?
  • Add a direct link from the rover to Earth to the plan: slow, but available for an hour every afternoon. Change ROUTE so the rover uses whichever link opens first. How much sooner does the answer arrive? (That’s the beginning of contact graph routing.)
  • The orbiter forgets a bundle as soon as it sends it on. What if the transmission is lost? Make the receiving node send back a small “got it” bundle, and have the sender keep its copy until one arrives: DTN’s version of Chapter 13’s acknowledgements, one hop at a time.
  • The Moon is 384,400 km away. Work out the round trip. Could you play an online game with someone there? Could TCP work, and what would it feel like?
