Looking back

It started with two kids, two bedroom windows and a flashlight. Alice wanted to ask Bob whether the coast was clear, and everything else followed from that one small wish, one problem at a time.

The flashlight couldn’t say much, so we invented codes. Light was unreliable, so we used a wire, and had to invent ways to keep the two ends in step. Both kids talked at once, so we invented turn-taking, frames and acknowledgments. Computers joined in, then bytes, pages and links. More houses meant sharing a cable, then switches, then packets with checks on them. More neighborhoods meant routers and addresses with parts. More programs meant ports. Unreliable datagrams led to reliable connections, and numbers led to names.

We invented all of it ourselves, and we called things whatever made sense at the time. The real internet was invented by thousands of people over about fifty years, and they faced the same problems, and very often came up with the same answers. They just gave them different names. It’s time to put the two side by side.

Some of our inventions match the real ones almost exactly. Chapter 11’s gossiping routers really are how RIP works, down to 16 meaning “unreachable”. Chapter 13’s timeout formula is TCP’s. Others are simplified: real IP addresses aren’t split neatly at a dot, and real Ethernet frames have a few more fields. But the reasons for every one of them are the reasons you now know.

Layers, for real

Back in Chapter 1, Alice split the job of signaling between Max, Grandpa Otto and her uncle, and each only had to understand their own part. The internet is organized the same way, into layers. Each layer adds its own header, reads only its own header at the other end, and trusts the layer below to deliver:

This is why the internet can change so much without falling apart. Wi-Fi replaced cables for most people without TCP noticing. Fiber replaced copper without IP noticing. The web was invented in 1989 on top of TCP, which was already fifteen years old. Each layer can be improved on its own, as long as it keeps its promises to the layers above and below.

You’ll often see a different diagram, with seven layers instead of five, called the OSI model. It comes from a rival set of standards from the 1980s that lost out to the internet’s, but its layer numbers stuck around. Network engineers still say “layer 2” for the link layer and “layer 3” for IP. Chapter 16 compares the two.

One click, every layer

Let’s follow a single, ordinary event all the way through: Alice opens a web page. Grandpa Otto has moved his web page to a real server on the internet, called grandpa-otto.example, and Alice types its address into her browser.

Notice the proportions. Alice’s request is 83 bytes, and it takes 54 bytes of envelopes to get it there: 14 for the frame, 20 for the packet and 20 for the connection. Plus 4 more for the frame’s CRC, and 8 before the frame to get the receiver in step, which network cards check and strip before anything else sees them. For a big download, with 1,460 bytes of data in every packet, the envelopes are only about 5% of the total. For tiny messages, they’re most of it.

A recording of network traffic is called a packet capture, and the program that makes one is a packet sniffer. The classic one is tcpdump, written in 1988 by Van Jacobson (yes, him again), Craig Leres and Steven McCanne. Wireshark reads the same files and shows them with a friendly interface, and it knows how to decode thousands of protocols.

Capturing live traffic needs administrator rights, because a sniffer can see everything that crosses your network card, including other people’s traffic on a shared network. Only capture on networks you own, or have permission to watch. These days, most of what you’d see is encrypted anyway: nearly all web traffic uses HTTPS, which wraps the HTTP layer in encryption so that a sniffer sees only scrambled bytes (Chapters 31 and 32).

What we left out

Part I invented a network that works, but the real internet has a few more tricks, and a few more problems. Here are some of the things Part II will add:

  • Where addresses come from. We handed them out by hand. Real computers ask for one when they join a network (Chapter 22).
  • Home routers that rewrite addresses. In our capture, Alice’s packets go out with her address, 192.168.1.3. In reality, her home router swaps that for its own public address on the way out, and swaps it back for the replies. That’s called NAT, and it’s why billions of devices can share far fewer addresses (Chapter 22).
  • Running out of addresses. 32 bits isn’t enough for the world. IPv6 has 128 (Chapter 25).
  • Routing between tens of thousands of networks, run by companies that don’t always trust each other (Chapter 24).
  • Secrets. Everything we built can be read, and changed, by Eve. Part II adds cryptography, and then HTTPS (Chapters 31 and 32).

What’s next?

That’s the end of Part I. You’ve invented a working computer network, from flashing lights to web pages, and you know why every piece of it is there.

In Part II, “Networks as They Are”, we stop inventing and start investigating. We’ll take each layer of the real internet apart, on your own computer: capturing real packets, reading real headers, and building real clients and servers from raw sockets. Chapter 16 starts where this one ends: with a proper toolkit for capturing and filtering live packets on your own network.

This lab has two programs. make_sample.py builds sample.pcap, a recording of the thirteen packets from this chapter’s journey. It builds every byte by hand, using real header layouts and real checksums, so that standard tools can read it. peel.py is a packet decoder, written from scratch, that peels each packet apart using this book’s words.

4. Capture your own

Now record something real. Start Chapter 7’s server, and in a second terminal start capturing traffic to its port on the loopback interface. This needs your password, because of sudo:

$ python3 ../ch07/dynserver.py $ sudo tcpdump -i lo0 -w mine.pcap port 7007 # on Linux, use -i lo instead of -i lo0

In a third terminal, fetch a page with curl http://localhost:7007/dice, then go back to terminal 2 and press Ctrl-C. Now peel your own capture:

$ python3 peel.py mine.pcap -v

You’ll see the handshake, your request, the reply and the goodbye, just like the sample. On a Mac, the first layer will say “loopback” instead of “frame”: these packets never left your computer, so they never needed card addresses.

Experiments

  • Damage the sample: change one byte in the middle of packet 8 (data = bytearray(open("sample.pcap","rb").read()); data[650] ^= 1, then write it back to a new file). Which check does peel.py say is bad? Which byte would you have to change to fool it?
  • Add ICMP to peel.py (protocol 1), then capture a ping and decode it. The type byte says whether it’s a question (8) or an answer (0).
  • Capture Chapter 12’s chess game (port 7064) and teach peel.py to print the moves.
  • Work out the envelope overhead for every packet in sample.pcap: header bytes divided by total bytes. Which packets are nearly all envelope?
