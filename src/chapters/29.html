Up to 48 hours

Grandpa Otto has decided he needs a website, for his vegetable garden. Bob has registered him a domain name, pointed it at a little web server on the Pi, and now they’re waiting.

B: The registrar’s website says changes can take “up to 48 hours to propagate across the internet”. A: Propagate? There’s nothing to propagate. The moment you saved it, CERN… sorry, your registrar’s name servers started giving out the new address. B: Then why can’t Grandpa see his site? A: Because his laptop asked yesterday, before you set it up, got “no such name”, and his internet provider’s resolver is remembering that answer. For as long as it was told it could. B: Who told it that? A: The name server, in its answer. It’s all in the message. Want to see one?

In Chapter 14, Alice and Bob invented a naming system: a tree of name servers, each responsible for one part of the name, and resolvers that walk down the tree and remember the answers. That’s the Domain Name System, DNS, almost exactly. This chapter looks at the real thing: its messages, byte by byte; the walk from the root to an answer, for real; the record types; caching and its famous myth; and how DNS is being made harder to fool and harder to snoop on. And we’ll build a resolver that can find any name on the internet, starting from nothing but the addresses of the root servers.

Who does the work

When a program wants an address, it doesn’t do the lookup itself. It asks the operating system’s stub resolver, a small piece of code that sends one question to a recursive resolver, and waits for the final answer. In Bob’s house, the recursive resolver the laptop asks is the router, which passes the question on to the internet provider’s resolvers. Or it can be a public one, like Cloudflare’s 1.1.1.1 or Google’s 8.8.8.8. The recursive resolver does the real work, asking the authoritative servers, the ones run by whoever owns each part of the name, and remembering everything it learns for the next person who asks.

A message, byte by byte

A DNS message is the same shape whether it’s a question or an answer: a 12-byte header, then four sections. The question says what’s being asked: a name, a type (A for an IPv4 address, AAAA for IPv6, and so on) and a class (always “IN”, for internet). The answer section holds the records that answer it. The authority section holds referrals: the NS records of the servers to ask next. And the additional section holds helpful extras, like the addresses of those servers. Each record has a name, a type, a class, a time to live in seconds, and its data.

Two tricks keep messages small. Names are written as labels, each with its length in front and a zero at the end: 04 info 04 cern 02 ch 00, so there’s no need for dots. And any name, or the end of one, that appeared earlier in the message is replaced by a two-byte pointer back to it. The answer never repeats info.cern.ch; it says c0 0c, “see byte 12”. Without compression, this answer would be 36 bytes longer.

The header’s flags tell the story of the exchange. qr means it’s a response. rd, “recursion desired”, was copied from the question: “please do the whole lookup for me”. ra says this server is willing to. An authoritative server would set aa instead, and a resolver that checked the answer’s signatures sets ad (more on that below). And the last four bits are the response code: NOERROR, or NXDOMAIN (“no such name”), or SERVFAIL (“I tried, and something went wrong”).

Remembering, and the propagation myth

Every record carries a TTL, set by the domain’s owner, and every resolver that stores the record counts it down. You can watch Bob’s router do it: ask the same question three times, ten seconds apart:

Now Bob’s “48 hours” makes sense, and so does its fix:

DNS changes don’t spread outward like ripples. Every resolver in the world simply keeps what it was told, for as long as it was told it could. The “48 hours” is the TTL of the records at the top of the tree, which rarely change (and a registrar being cautious). The trick professionals use is to lower a record’s TTL a day before changing it, so that when the change comes, the whole world picks it up within minutes. And Grandpa Otto’s problem was a negative answer, cached for the SOA’s 900 seconds. Fifteen minutes later, his garden was on the internet.

UDP, TCP, and size

DNS usually uses UDP, port 53: one datagram for the question, one for the answer (Chapter 26). Originally, answers were limited to 512 bytes, so that they’d never be fragmented. That’s why there are only 13 root server names: 13 is how many fit in 512 bytes. Anything bigger was cut short, with the tc (“truncated”) flag set, and the resolver asked again over TCP, where there’s no limit.

Modern resolvers add a pseudo-record to every question, EDNS (that’s the OPT record at the end of the message above), which says how big an answer they can take. Today almost everyone says 1,232 bytes: the most that fits in a single packet on nearly any IPv6 path, even through tunnels. Ask for something big with a small limit, and you can watch truncation happen:

$ dig +bufsize=512 +ignore @1.1.1.1 DNSKEY org | grep -E "flags:|MSG SIZE" ;; flags: qr ⟦1:tc⟧ rd ra; QUERY: 1, ANSWER: 0, AUTHORITY: 0, ADDITIONAL: 1 ;; MSG SIZE rcvd: 32 $ dig +tcp @1.1.1.1 DNSKEY org | grep -E "flags:|MSG SIZE" ;; flags: qr rd ra ad; QUERY: 1, ANSWER: 5, AUTHORITY: 0, ADDITIONAL: 1 ;; MSG SIZE rcvd: ⟦2:1028⟧ 1 | “Truncated”: the answer didn’t fit in 512 bytes, so the server sent none of it. (+ignore tells dig not to retry over TCP by itself.) 2 | Over TCP, the whole 1,028-byte answer: the signing keys of the org domain.

Can you believe the answer?

Chapter 14 ended with Eve poisoning a cache, by sending a fake answer before the real one arrived. It’s the oldest attack on DNS, and in 2008 a researcher named Dan Kaminsky found a way to make it devastatingly easy: ask the victim resolver about thousands of made-up names in a domain (1.bank.example, 2.bank.example…), and flood it with fake answers for each, all containing a fake referral for the whole domain. Every attempt was a new race, and one would eventually win. The whole internet’s DNS software was patched in a single, secretly coordinated day. The patch made the race much harder to win: a fake answer must now match not only the 16-bit ID, but also the random source port the question came from, which makes it about 65,000 times harder. Some resolvers also randomize the capital letters in the name they ask about (iNfO.CeRn.cH), and check that the answer copies them exactly.

The real fix is to make answers impossible to forge at all, with signatures. That’s DNSSEC:

DNSSEC protects against forged answers, but it doesn’t hide anything: the questions and answers still cross the network in plain view, and anyone on the path, like Eve on Bob’s Wi-Fi or a provider selling browsing data, can read every name you look up. So there are also ways to encrypt DNS: DNS over TLS, on port 853, and DNS over HTTPS, which hides DNS messages inside ordinary web traffic on port 443. Why does DNS use UDP, and not TCP, which would never lose an answer?

Because a DNS lookup is one question and one answer, and they usually fit in one packet each. TCP would need a whole handshake first, a round trip before the question could even be sent (Chapter 27), and every web page needs dozens of lookups. A lost UDP question costs a second or so before the resolver asks again, which is rare. The price is that UDP’s source address is easy to fake, which is what makes cache poisoning possible, and why DNS servers get used to amplify attacks: a small question with a faked source address, and a big answer sent to the victim.

Before DNS, every computer on the ARPANET had a copy of one file, HOSTS.TXT, listing every other computer’s name and address, and downloaded a new copy from Stanford Research Institute every so often. (Your computer still has its descendant: /etc/hosts.) By 1983 it was changing faster than it could be copied, and Paul Mockapetris designed DNS to replace it. Its basic design has hardly changed since.

The root zone was finally signed with DNSSEC in 2010. Its most important key is kept in two guarded facilities in the United States, and it’s used a few times a year, at public “key signing ceremonies” where trusted people from around the world, each holding a piece of what’s needed to unlock it, meet in a secure room, on camera, to sign the next few months of keys.
