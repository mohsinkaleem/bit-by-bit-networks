Half a minute of silence

Bob is spending the weekend at Alice’s university, revising for his exams in the library while she works. Alice has a part-time job with the university’s network team, helping Carol, the engineer who runs the campus network. Halfway through the afternoon, Bob’s video call with his study group freezes. Thirty-five seconds later it comes back, as if nothing had happened.

B: My call just died for half a minute. Was that you? A: A digger. It went straight through the fibre between the library and the main building. Carol’s out there now, shouting at a man in a hi-vis jacket. B: But everything’s working again. A: There’s another way round: from the library to the lab building, and from there to the main building. The routers found it by themselves. B: Like the gossiping routers we invented, years ago? C: Not quite. Mine don’t gossip, they draw maps. And every one of them has the same map. B: Then why did it take thirty-five seconds? C: Ah. That’s my favourite complaint.

In Chapter 11 we invented routers, and a way for them to share what they know. This chapter is about how real routers do it inside one organization, like a university, a company or an internet provider. First, how a router uses its table, with a rule called longest-prefix match. Then, how the table gets filled in, by the old protocol that works like ours, RIP, and the map-drawing one that replaced it, OSPF. And we’ll build a small network of real OSPF routers inside Grandpa Otto’s Raspberry Pi, and cut its cables.

The longest match wins

Look at Bob’s table again. A packet to the Pi, 192.168.1.4, matches three routes: the default route (which matches everything), 192.168.1.0/24, and the route for 192.168.1.4 alone. Which one does the laptop use?

The most specific one. A route is a prefix: “every address whose first 24 bits are these”. The more bits a route pins down, the fewer addresses it covers, and the more it knows about where they are. So the rule is: of all the routes that match, use the one with the longest prefix. Here it is for an address on Carol’s campus:

This one rule does a surprising amount of work. The default route, 0.0.0.0/0, is simply the shortest possible prefix, so it loses to everything and catches whatever’s left. A /32 names a single computer, so it beats everything. And in between, you can make exceptions: the lab building handles all of 10.1.0.0/16, except the library’s /24, except one server that moved. A router can’t check every route one by one: an internet provider’s router holds about a million, and has a few nanoseconds per packet. This chapter’s lab stores them in a trie, a tree with one level per bit, and finds the answer by walking down it, one bit of the address at a time, remembering the last route it passed. That takes at most 32 steps, however big the table is. Real routers do the same kind of walk in special chips, or look at every route at once with a kind of memory called TCAM, which compares an address with every stored prefix in parallel.

Classes, and why they went away

It wasn’t always like this. When IP addresses were first handed out, in 1981, there was no slash. The first few bits of an address said how big its network was:

It was simple, and it was a disaster. A class C was too small for almost anyone, and a class A too big for almost everyone, so everybody asked for a class B, and by the early 1990s they were running out. Worse, every network needed its own entry in every router on the internet, and the routers of the day were running out of memory. So in 1993 the classes were abolished, and replaced with classless inter-domain routing, or CIDR (said “cider”): the slash notation from Chapter 20, where the network part can be any length. Organizations could be given exactly the size they needed. And the tables could shrink:

Aggregation only works if addresses are handed out in blocks that follow the shape of the network: an internet provider gets a big block, and gives its customers pieces of it. Every time a customer leaves and takes its addresses along, or a network announces its pieces separately to control which way traffic comes in, the table grows. That’s why the internet’s table has kept growing ever since, and is now close to a million routes. (Chapter 24 will show what happens when someone announces a more specific route for addresses that aren’t theirs. Longest-prefix match is happy to believe them.)

Who writes the table?

Routes get into a table in three ways. Connected routes appear by themselves: the moment an interface gets an address, the computer knows its network is right there (proto kernel). Static routes are typed in by someone: sudo ip route add 10.9.0.0/16 via 192.168.1.50. And dynamic routes are filled in by a program that talks to other routers, using a routing protocol.

Static routes are fine for a house, where there’s only one way out. They’re hopeless for Carol’s campus, with dozens of routers and several paths between most buildings. Nobody could keep them all up to date, and when the digger struck, somebody would have had to rush round retyping them. So the routers work it out among themselves, with a protocol that runs inside one organization, called an interior gateway protocol. There are two kinds.

Gossip, revisited: RIP

The first kind is the one we invented in Chapter 11. Every router tells its neighbours its whole table, “network 4 is 2 hops away from me”, and each neighbour adds one and keeps the best offer. It’s called distance vector routing, and the real version is the Routing Information Protocol, RIP, which really does all the things ours did. It sends its table every 30 seconds, to UDP port 520. It counts hops. And it has the same weakness: when a network disappears, the bad news spreads slowly, and routers can count to infinity, where infinity is 16. Split horizon, which we tried in Chapter 11’s lab, is in the standard, along with “poison reverse” (tell the neighbour you learned a route from that it’s 16 hops away, rather than saying nothing).

RIP is still found in small networks, and it’s a lovely thing to learn from. But look at what it can’t do. A network more than 15 hops across is unreachable by definition. It counts every hop the same, whether it’s a fibre or a piece of wet string. And after a failure, it can take minutes to settle. For anything bigger than a few routers, people use the other kind.

Drawing the whole map: link state

Instead of telling its neighbours what it’s heard, each router tells everyone only what it knows for sure: which cables it has, and what each one costs. That’s a link-state advertisement, or LSA. Then every router collects everyone’s, and builds a map of the whole network. Getting each advertisement to every router, without a routing table (which is what we’re trying to build), uses a trick called flooding:

Each advertisement carries a sequence number. When a router’s cables change, it sends a new advertisement with the next number, and everyone replaces the old one. Advertisements also have an age, and are sent again every 30 minutes even if nothing changed, so that a router that disappears without a word is eventually forgotten.

The cost of a cable is chosen by the network’s owner. It’s usually worked out from its speed: OSPF divides a “reference speed” by the cable’s speed, so if the reference is 100 Gb/s, a 10 Gb/s fibre costs 10, and an old 1 Gb/s cable costs 100. That’s the campus in these figures: the old cable from the lab to the sports centre is ten times as expensive as the others. The best route isn’t the one with fewest hops, but the one with the lowest total cost.

Dijkstra’s shortest paths

With the map in hand, each router works out the cheapest path to every other router, using an algorithm invented by the Dutch computer scientist Edsger Dijkstra in 1956. It grows outward from the router itself, like a ripple, always settling the closest router it hasn’t settled yet, which is guaranteed to be as close as it will ever get. The result is a shortest-path tree: the best way from here to everywhere. Only the first step of each path matters for the routing table, because the next router will work out the rest of the path for itself, from the same map, and come to the same answer.

The library has two equally good ways to reach 10.0.23.0/30, and uses both. That’s called equal-cost multipath, or ECMP, and big networks rely on it to use all their cables at once. But should the router alternate, sending one packet each way?

No: the two paths might take slightly different times, and packets of the same conversation would arrive out of order. TCP (Chapter 28) takes out-of-order packets as a sign that something was lost, and slows down. So routers pick the path for each packet by hashing its addresses and port numbers: every packet of one conversation gets the same answer, and goes the same way, while different conversations spread across both. It’s also why traceroute sometimes shows different routers answering for the same hop (Chapter 21): each probe uses a different port, so it can take a different path.

Six tenths of a second. When a cable is pulled out, the routers at both ends see their interface go down, and each floods a new advertisement straight away. Every router runs Dijkstra again, and the new paths are in place before most people notice. So why did Bob lose thirty-five seconds?

Because on the real campus, the fibre doesn’t plug straight into the routers at both ends. It goes through a switch in a cupboard on the way. When the digger cut the fibre, the router’s own cable, to the switch, was fine. Its interface stayed up. It had no way of knowing that nothing on the other side was listening any more, except that the Hellos stopped. And a router only gives up on a silent neighbour after the dead interval: 40 seconds, four missed Hellos. Alice’s script can do that too. It leaves the cable “plugged in”, but throws away everything sent across it:

Thirty-five seconds, just like Bob’s call (the main router’s last Hello had arrived five seconds before the cable went quiet). You could say hello more often, but OSPF’s Hellos are handled by the router’s ordinary processor, which is busy with other things. So real networks add a separate, tiny protocol whose only job is to notice this: Bidirectional Forwarding Detection, or BFD, which sends its own tiny hellos as often as every 50 milliseconds, often handled by the network card itself, and tells OSPF the moment they stop. Carol’s complaint is that the library link was the last one without it.

Big networks go further, and work out a backup path in advance, so that the router can switch to it the moment a link fails, before anyone has even flooded the news. It’s called fast reroute, and it can mend a broken path in less time than it takes to blink.

Bigger networks: areas, and IS-IS

Flooding and Dijkstra get expensive when a network has thousands of routers: every change is flooded to all of them, and all of them rerun the algorithm. So OSPF lets a big network be split into areas. Routers only have the full map of their own area. At the edge of an area, an “area border router” tells the rest of the network just the networks inside, not the detailed map, and ideally aggregates them into a few short prefixes, like the internet provider in the aggregation figure. All areas connect to a central one, area 0, the backbone, which is the “area 0.0.0.0” in BIRD’s output. A digger in one area barely troubles the others.

Many of the biggest internet providers use a close cousin of OSPF instead, called IS-IS (“intermediate system to intermediate system”, which is what the international standards of the 1980s called a router). It floods link states and runs Dijkstra just the same. It came from a rival family of network protocols that lost to TCP/IP, but it carries IP routes perfectly well, and because it doesn’t run on top of IP itself, it could carry IPv6 routes with hardly any changes.

The ARPANET, the internet’s ancestor, started out with distance-vector routing in 1969, and suffered from its slow convergence and loops. In 1979, it switched to link-state routing, with each switching computer flooding the delays on its lines, and running Dijkstra’s algorithm. RIP followed a different path: it began as a program called routed in BSD Unix in 1982, and was only written down as a standard in 1988, after it was already everywhere. OSPF was designed in the late 1980s, and its current version for IPv4, OSPFv2, was published in 1998. OSPFv3 does the same job for IPv6.

Dijkstra said he designed his algorithm in about twenty minutes, sitting on a café terrace in Amsterdam with his fiancée, without pencil or paper. He was looking for a problem that non-computer-people could understand, to show off a new computer, and chose the shortest road between two Dutch cities.

What’s next?

Inside the university, every router trusts every other one, they all share one complete map, and they all agree on what “shortest” means. None of that is true between organizations. The internet provider on the other side of Carol’s gateway doesn’t want to show her its map, and it certainly doesn’t pick routes by the cheapest cable: it picks them by who pays whom. Chapter 24 is about the protocol that holds the internet’s tens of thousands of networks together, BGP, which is part routing protocol and part diplomacy.

Three programs. lpm.py is a routing table with longest-prefix match, and can load your computer’s real one. linkstate.py floods advertisements, runs Dijkstra, and reads a real OSPF capture. ospf_box.sh builds the three real OSPF routers from the story, if you have a Linux computer.
