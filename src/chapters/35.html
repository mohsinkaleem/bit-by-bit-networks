Slugs at midnight

Something is eating Grandpa Otto’s lettuces at night. He has taped a camera to the Pi, pointed it at the vegetable bed, and asked Bob to make it so he can watch from Alice’s house, where he plays cards on Thursdays.

B: The Pi has a private address, 192.168.1.4. From Alice’s house, that address means nothing: it’s some other house’s printer, or nobody. A: You could forward a port on Grandpa’s router to the camera, like we did for the game in Chapter 22. B: Then everyone on the internet can watch the lettuces. Including Eve. E: Especially Eve. A: So don’t send packets to the camera across the internet. Send them to Grandpa’s router, in a sealed envelope. Inside the envelope, write the camera’s private address. Something at Grandpa’s end opens it, and delivers what’s inside, as if it had come from his own house. B: A packet inside a packet? A: A tunnel.

A tunnel carries packets of one network inside packets of another. It’s how a laptop on a café’s Wi-Fi joins its office network, how a company joins two offices into one network, how cloud providers give every customer a private network on shared machines, and, with encryption added, what people mean by a VPN, a virtual private network. This chapter builds tunnels on the Pi, and then a WireGuard VPN client in Python that talks to the real WireGuard inside Linux.

A packet inside a packet

The simplest tunnels don’t even encrypt. GRE, generic routing encapsulation, puts a 4-byte header and a new IP header in front of a packet, and that’s all. This chapter’s tunnel_box.sh builds two “houses” on the Pi (two network namespaces, which Chapter 36 explains; think of them as two separate computers), joins them with a cable playing the part of the internet, and runs a tunnel between them. Here’s one ping, captured on the “internet”:

Tunnels you can’t read: VPNs

Add Chapter 31’s cryptography to the wrapping, and a tunnel becomes a VPN. Three families do almost all of it:

  • IPsec, from the mid-1990s: built into every operating system and router, used to join offices together, and quietly behind your phone’s “Wi-Fi calling”, which tunnels calls to your mobile network over whatever Wi-Fi you’re on.
  • TLS-based VPNs, like OpenVPN, and most company “SSL VPNs”: the same TLS as Chapter 32, carrying packets instead of web pages.
  • WireGuard, from 2016: small, fast and simple, built into Linux since 2020, and underneath many commercial VPN apps and “mesh” VPNs.

WireGuard is the easiest to understand, so let’s build one.

WireGuard

WireGuard looks like SSH’s keys more than like TLS’s certificates. Each computer has a key pair, and each side is simply told the other’s public key in advance. There are no certificate authorities, no usernames, no choice of ciphers (it uses X25519, ChaCha20-Poly1305 and a hash called BLAKE2s, and that’s that) and almost no settings. The whole thing is about 4,000 lines of code, small enough for one person to check; IPsec and OpenVPN implementations are many times bigger.

The peer list isn’t only a list of who may connect: it’s a routing table too, and a firewall.

To give Grandpa his camera, Bob sets Grandpa’s router to forward UDP port 51820 to the Pi, and gives the Pi his laptop’s public key. That forwarded port is safe to leave open: WireGuard answers nobody it doesn’t know, so to Eve, port 51820 looks exactly like a closed one. (Commercial “mesh” VPNs go one step further, and use Chapter 22’s STUN tricks to punch through both routers’ NATs, so nobody has to forward anything.)

IPsec, briefly

IPsec does the same job the committee way. A handshake protocol called IKE (on UDP port 500) checks certificates or passwords and agrees on keys, and the packets themselves travel as ESP, a protocol of its own, next to TCP and UDP:

Notice the last row. ESP has no port numbers, so the NAT in a home router (Chapter 22) has nothing to tell connections apart by. When IKE notices a NAT in the way, both ends switch to wrapping ESP in UDP, on port 4500: the same lesson QUIC learned in Chapter 33, that the internet only really carries TCP and UDP.

Whole networks inside UDP

GRE and WireGuard carry IP packets. Some tunnels carry whole Ethernet frames instead, so that computers in different buildings, or on different physical machines, believe they’re plugged into the same switch. The most common is VXLAN, which wraps frames in UDP, on port 4789:

What tunnels cost

Every tunnel spends some of each packet on wrapping, and the packets inside must get smaller to make room. This is why tunnels are the classic cause of the strangest network problem there is: small things work, and big things hang. A web page’s first few packets are small, so the connection opens fine; then the big ones don’t fit, get dropped somewhere in the middle, and the “too big” warnings (ICMP, Chapter 21) that should have come back were blocked by a firewall. Chapter 38 hunts one down.

WireGuard and IPsec send their packets over UDP (or straight over IP). OpenVPN can use TCP, and so can the “SSL VPNs” many companies use, because TCP gets through firewalls that block everything else. Why is a VPN over TCP a bad idea, if you can avoid it?

Because most of what travels inside the tunnel is TCP too. When a packet is lost, the outer TCP connection stops everything and resends it (Chapter 33’s head-of-line blocking), and slows down (Chapter 28). Meanwhile, the inner TCP connections notice their data is late, assume it was lost, and send it again: more data into a tunnel that’s already stalled and slowing down. Two sets of timers fight each other, and throughput can collapse, a problem known as “TCP meltdown”. Over UDP, the tunnel just loses the packet, and only the inner TCP deals with it, exactly as it would without a tunnel.

Jason Donenfeld, a security researcher, wrote WireGuard in 2015 and 2016, having found existing VPN software huge and hard to trust. Linus Torvalds called it “a work of art” compared with the alternatives, and it went into Linux in March 2020. It’s underneath many of the VPN apps people buy today.

VXLAN was published in 2014 by a group of companies including VMware and Cisco, to solve a data-centre problem: VLANs (Chapter 18) allow only 4,094 separate networks, and cloud providers have millions of customers. Its successor, Geneve, adds room for extra information in each packet, and is used by several cloud providers and container networks.

Experiments

  • In wg_peer.py, send the same handshake initiation twice. The Pi answers the first and ignores the second: its timestamp is no newer than one it has already seen. Why does that matter, if Eve records the message?
  • Change one byte of a transport data message before sending it. What does the Pi send back? (Look at wg show’s transfer counters, too.) What does it send back to a handshake from a key it doesn’t know?
  • Ping through the tunnel with bigger and bigger packets. wg_peer.py builds the ICMP by hand: add a size option, and find the largest that makes it through. How does it compare with wg0’s MTU of 1,420?
  • Run VXLAN inside WireGuard: in tunnel_box.sh, what would have to change? Use the widget to work out the MTU first.
