Two windows, one street

What does it actually mean for two computers to “talk”? Take away the screens and the cables and the blinking lights, and it comes down to something simple: one side does something the other side can notice, and both sides agreed beforehand on what that something means.

That’s all there is to it. Everything in this book, from the Wi-Fi in your house to the undersea cables that carry the internet between continents, is an elaborate answer to one question: how do we make something get noticed, reliably, a long way away, by the right someone, and mean the right thing?

We’re going to build our answer one piece at a time, and we’ll start somewhere that has nothing to do with computers.

Meet Alice and Bob. They’re best friends, they live across a narrow street from each other, and their bedroom windows face each other. Their parents have a strict rule: lights out and no phones after nine. Alice and Bob have a rule of their own: a conversation isn’t over just because the grown-ups say so.

Alice has a flashlight.

Is the coast clear?

Here’s the simplest conversation Alice and Bob could have. At school they make a plan: they’ll meet at the treehouse on Saturday morning, but only if Alice’s mom forgets to sign her up for swimming lessons. Alice won’t know until tonight. So they agree that at 9:30, Bob will look out his window. If Alice’s flashlight is on, the treehouse is a go. If it’s off, it isn’t.

At 9:30 Bob peeks through his curtains, and there it is: a little circle of light in Alice’s window. Treehouse on Saturday.

Look at what just happened. Alice sent Bob a message, but the message didn’t contain any words. The light didn’t say “the treehouse is on”. It only said “on”, and the meaning came from the plan they made beforehand. Bob didn’t have to ask the question out loud, because they had already agreed what the question was.

That kind of agreement is the most important idea in this book, and it has a name: a protocol. A protocol is a set of rules that both sides agree on in advance, so that a signal can mean something. “Light at 9:30 means the treehouse is on” is a protocol. It’s a tiny one, but it’s real.

The answer to a single yes-or-no question is the smallest amount of information you can send, and we call it one bit. It’s the atom of communication. Everything else, from a text message to a song to a movie, is built out of lots and lots of bits.

The word protocol comes from diplomacy, where it means the etiquette for how officials greet each other and exchange documents. Computer scientists borrowed it in the 1960s, and it fits: a network protocol is etiquette for machines.

Bit is short for “binary digit”. The statistician John Tukey coined the word, and Claude Shannon made it famous in his 1948 paper that founded information theory. Shannon gave “information” a precise meaning: one bit is what you learn from the answer to a yes/no question when both answers were equally likely.

Signalling with lights is much older than computers. Navies used shuttered signal lamps well into the twentieth century, and many ships still carry one as a backup for when the radio fails.

The trouble with darkness

The treehouse plan works so well that they use it again the next week. This time, Bob looks out at 9:30 and sees… nothing. Alice’s window is dark.

Does that mean no treehouse? Probably. But maybe Alice fell asleep early. Maybe the batteries died. Maybe her dad took the flashlight away. Maybe she’s standing at the window right now, frantically clicking a broken switch. From across the street, all of those look exactly the same: dark.

It’s a sneaky problem, and it turns up all over networking. When “no” and “nothing” look the same, the receiver can’t tell an answer from a failure. Silence is ambiguous.

Alice fixes it with some colored cellophane. She makes two covers for the flashlight, one green and one red. Green means yes and red means no. Darkness now means “Alice isn’t saying anything”, which is a completely different thing.

Now if Bob sees a dark window at 9:30, he knows something went wrong and can ask Alice about it in the morning. What matters is that he won’t mistake a failure for an answer.

That’s a bigger step than it looks. The window now has three possible states: green, red and dark. Two of them carry information, and the third means “no signal at all”. The same idea will come back when we swap the flashlight for electricity. There, it’ll be the difference between a positive voltage, a negative voltage and a wire that’s been cut.

One flash at a time

Alice and Bob quickly want more than one question a night. So at school they write a list:

  1. Did you finish the math homework?
  2. Is your cousin still visiting?
  3. Did you see the new episode?
  4. Want to trade lunches tomorrow?

At 9:30, Alice will answer all four in order, one flash per question, and Bob will write the answers down.

Alice’s answers are yes, yes, no, yes. So she flashes green… and then green again. How is Bob supposed to know there were two greens? From across the street, green followed by green looks like one long green. Did she answer one question or two?

They fix it by putting a short moment of darkness between flashes: flash, dark, flash, dark. Each flash is now its own separate answer, and the gaps work like the spaces between words.

With gaps, Alice can send as many answers in a row as she likes. A sequence of bits like this is called a bit stream, and Bob decodes it by counting: first flash, first question; second flash, second question; and so on. Sending bits one after another over a single channel like this is called serial communication. Alice could instead use four flashlights at once, one per question, which would be parallel communication. Over long distances serial wins, because it only needs one channel, and that trade-off will keep coming up.

The gap trick depends on Bob’s sense of timing. What happens if Alice speeds up and her gaps get shorter and shorter? What if Bob blinks at the wrong moment? Keep those questions in mind, because they’re what Chapter 2 is about.

Twenty questions, minus fifteen

Answering a list of questions written in advance is useful, but it isn’t really a conversation. What Alice wants is to send words, any words she likes, including ones Bob isn’t expecting. Can yes-or-no flashes do that?

They can, using the same trick as the game Twenty Questions. The questions don’t have to be yes/no; they just need exactly two possible answers. “Is your letter in the first half or the second half of the alphabet?” has two answers, so one flash can answer it: red for the first half, green for the second.

There’s one snag. The alphabet has 26 letters, and 26 doesn’t keep splitting evenly (26 → 13 → 6½?). So Alice and Bob add six extra symbols to make an even 32: a space, a period, a comma, a question mark, an exclamation mark, and a squiggle, ~, that they don’t have a use for yet. (They will. It saves the day in Chapter 3.)

Why 32? Because 32 can be cut exactly in half five times in a row: 32 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1. Each cut takes one question and each question takes one flash, so five flashes are enough to pick out any symbol. Here it is with the letter K:

Counting on two fingers

There’s something neat hiding in those answers. When we found K, the answers were left, right, left, right, left. Write left as 0 and right as 1, and you get 01010. That happens to be how you write the number 10 in binary. And K is the 10th symbol on our list, if you count from zero the way computers do: A is 0, B is 1, C is 2 … K is 10.

That’s no coincidence. To see why, take a closer look at how we write ordinary numbers. You’ve been doing it since you were little, so you probably don’t think about it anymore. Take 7,305. The digits mean 7 thousands, 3 hundreds, 0 tens and 5 ones. Each position is worth ten times the position to its right, and the digit in it says how many of that value to add. That’s why it’s called base 10, or decimal.

Binary, or base 2, works exactly the same way, but with 2 instead of 10. Each position is worth twice the one to its right: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and so on. And because each place can only hold “none” or “one” of its value, the only digits are 0 and 1.

So 01010 means no 16s, one 8, no 4s, one 2 and no 1s: 8 + 2 = 10. When Alice picks the right half, she’s really saying “add this place’s value”. The first question decides whether to add 16, the second whether to add 8, and so on down to 1.

How many bits is enough?

Every extra bit doubles the number of different things you can say. One bit gives you two possibilities, 0 or 1. Two bits give you four: 00, 01, 10 and 11. Three bits give you eight. You can picture each extra bit as one more fork in the road:

BitsPossibilitiesEnough for…
12yes or no
24the four seasons
38the days of the week (with one left over)
416not quite the alphabet…
532the alphabet plus six extras
8256upper- and lowercase letters, digits, punctuation (Chapter 5)
32about 4.3 billionevery address on the original internet (Chapter 20)

With n bits you get 2n possibilities. Going the other way, to pick one thing out of N you need enough bits that 2bits is at least N. The 26 letters need 5 bits, because 4 bits only get you to 16. Mathematicians write that as log2(26) ≈ 4.7 and round up to 5.

The codebook

Alice and Bob don’t want to play Twenty Questions in their heads for every letter. So they write out every symbol with its 5-bit code, once and for all, and each keeps a copy by the window:

The codebook is part of their protocol, too. It only works because they both have the same copy. If Bob’s copy listed the letters in a different order, every message would come out as gibberish.

Now Alice can say anything. To send “HI BOB”, she looks up each symbol and flashes its five bits:

Bob watches, writes down the bits, splits them into groups of five and looks each group up. 00111 is H. 01000 is I. 11010 is a space. And so on.

That’s thirty flashes for six characters. It’s slow, but it works, and it can carry any message they can spell, which is a big deal. Our flashlight started out answering one yes/no question, and now it’s a general-purpose messaging system.

What happens if Bob misses one flash in the middle of “HI BOB”, say because he sneezed? Every group of five after that point is off by one, and the rest of the message turns into nonsense. How could Alice and Bob notice that it happened, or recover from it? Hold on to that thought; we’ll deal with lost bits in Chapters 3 and 10.

Alice and Bob’s codebook is very close to a real one. In the 1870s, the French telegraph engineer Émile Baudot built a telegraph system around a 5-bit code. An improved version, known as ITA2 or just “Baudot code”, ran the world’s teleprinters for most of the twentieth century. It squeezed more than 32 symbols out of 5 bits with a trick: two special codes switched between a “letters” mode and a “figures” mode, a bit like the Shift key on a keyboard.

The even older Morse code took a different approach. It gives common letters short codes (E is a single dot) and rare letters long ones (Q is dash-dash-dot-dash), which makes typical messages shorter. That’s an early form of compression. The catch is that the codes have different lengths, so Morse needs pauses of different lengths to show where each letter ends.

Modern computers use 8-bit codes, which leave room for lowercase letters, digits and punctuation. We’ll switch to those in Chapter 5.

The night shift

The system works, but Alice has a lot on her plate. She has to decide what to say, turn every letter into five bits with the codebook, then flash each bit in the right color with a neat little gap afterwards. It’s hard to think about what you’re writing while you’re counting flashes.

So Alice gets help. Her little brother Max is great with the codebook. Their grandpa Otto used to be a signalman in the navy, and he loves nothing more than working a lamp. They split the job three ways:

  • Alice writes the message and hands it to Max one letter at a time.
  • Max looks up each letter and hands Grandpa Otto five bits on a slip of paper.
  • Grandpa Otto flashes each bit, green or red, followed by a dark gap.

Across the street, Bob’s household does the same jobs in reverse. His uncle Ray, a night owl, watches Alice’s window and writes down 1s and 0s. Bob’s sister Nia collects them five at a time and looks up the letters. Bob just gets to read the message.

Look closely at who knows what. Grandpa Otto doesn’t know the codebook at all; he just flashes whatever bits he’s handed. Max doesn’t need to know anything about lamps, and he doesn’t care what the message says. Alice never has to think about bits. Each person only needs to understand their own job, plus how to hand things to the person above or below them.

This way of splitting up a hard problem is called layering, and every real network is built from layers. Each layer does one job and only talks to the layers directly above and below it. Notice the dashed lines across the street in the last step, too. Every layer has a partner on the other side who speaks its language, and each of those pairs has its own little protocol.

Foggy, with a chance of bells

Here’s where layering really pays off. One night a thick fog rolls in, and Bob’s family can’t see Alice’s window at all. The flashlight is useless.

Grandpa Otto has an idea. He fetches Alice’s bicycle, which has a bell and a squeaky rubber horn. A ding means 1 and a honk means 0. Across the street, Uncle Ray listens instead of watching.

Here’s the lovely part: nobody else has to change anything. Max still hands Otto five bits at a time. Nia still gets five bits at a time from Ray. Alice and Bob don’t even find out there was fog. Only the bottom layer changed. Because each layer depends only on what its neighbors hand it, you can swap a layer out without the others noticing.

We’ll rely on this idea over and over. Real networks swap their bottom layer all the time: the same web page can reach you over copper wire, fiber-optic glass, Wi-Fi or a satellite link, and your web browser never knows the difference.

What’s next?

Our flashlight network works, but it has real problems. Alice and Bob need a clear line of sight and no fog. Any neighbor who can see Alice’s window can read every message, and there’s a neighbor named Eve who is very nosy, which will matter eventually. And it’s slow. A person can manage maybe two flashes a second before mistakes creep in.

In the next chapter we’ll string a wire between the two houses, swap light for electricity, and replace the humans with a machine that can send thousands of bits a second. It’ll turn out that keeping time is harder than it sounds.
