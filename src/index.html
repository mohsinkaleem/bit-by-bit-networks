Bit by Bit

computer networks, built from scratch

by Mohsin Kaleem

It starts with two kids, two bedroom windows and a flashlight. One problem at a time, Alice and Bob invent everything they need to talk: wires, codes, turn-taking, addresses, switches, routers, packets, retries and names. By the end of Part I you’ll have reinvented Ethernet, IP, UDP, TCP and DNS yourself, and seen why each one had to exist.

How the book is laid out

Part I

Networks as They Might Have Been

We invent a network from nothing. Every chapter starts with something that doesn’t work yet and ends with a fix, which is usually the same fix real engineers found.

Part II

Networks as They Are

Meet the real protocols we reinvented: Ethernet, Wi-Fi, IPv4 and IPv6, TCP, DNS, HTTP, TLS and QUIC, and the boxes in the middle. You’ll capture and take apart real packets on your own machine, and build clients for TLS 1.3, HTTP/3 and WireGuard from scratch.

Part III

Networks as They May Be

Where networking is heading: networks you program (and programs inside the kernel), the internet in orbit, messages to Mars, locks a quantum computer can’t pick, networks that ask for what instead of where, and villages that build their own. Real satellites’ orbits, a real post-quantum handshake, and a real named-data testbed.

Why build it from scratch?

Because every piece exists for a reason

A network protocol can look like an arbitrary pile of fields and flags. Once you’ve hit the problem each field solves, it reads more like a diary of past mistakes.

Because you remember what you invent

If you work out why a switch learns addresses, or why TCP needs three messages to say hello, you won’t have to memorize it.

Because it’s more fun

There are flashlights, fog, nosy neighbors, a grandpa who used to be a ship’s signalman, and far too many collisions.

Doing the labs

Every chapter ends with a Try it yourself section: a small Python program to run, change and break. You need Python 3.9 or newer (macOS already has it; check with python3 --version) and a terminal. The labs use only Python’s standard library, so there’s nothing to install. From Part II on, the labs also use real networking tools such as tcpdump, dig and Wireshark.

The lab files are in the labs/ folder next to this site, and each code listing has a download link.

Each chapter also has an At the command line box, pointing to real commands that show its ideas on your own computer. They’re collected in the Command-Line Field Guide, with examples for both macOS and Linux and real output explained line by line.
